Tracking a body that has no up

Why standard pose models collapse in microgravity, why the fix is a choice of rotation representation before it is a choice of model, and the end-to-end architecture for orientation-agnostic 3D Human Mesh Recovery measured against the payload rack rather than the floor.

Repo sih-har-isro Branch main Status HMR stubbed, disabled Chance floor 0.125

“Another challenge is that standard 2D or ground-based 3D posture models fail because astronauts do not have a fixed ‘up’ or ‘down’ orientation. The AI model should use orientation-agnostic 3D Human Mesh Recovery (HMR) to track the astronaut’s body relative to the payload rack, not the floor.”

Problem statement — optional challenge
01

The requirement contains three claims, not one

They have different answers, and conflating them is why this challenge usually gets handled badly.

Read closely and the statement asserts a failure, demands a reference frame, and prescribes a method. Only the first two are requirements. The third is a suggested means.

#ClaimKindOur status
1 2D and ground-based 3D pose models fail without a fixed up Assertion Confirmed, measured
2 Track the body relative to the payload rack, not the floor Requirement Done, in 2D
3 Use orientation-agnostic 3D HMR to do it Prescribed method Stubbed, disabled

Claim 1 we tested rather than assumed. Claim 2 is satisfied today by a 2D solution. Claim 3 is the gap, and this document is the architecture and plan for closing it — including an honest account of what it buys and what it does not.

02

What actually fails, measured

The failure mode is worse than degraded accuracy. The detector stops returning anything at all.

We rendered the same protocol at a range of crew roll angles and counted how often MediaPipe returned a pose at all. This is detection rate, not joint accuracy — the question is whether the system sees a person.

Crew roll MediaPipe alone With upright_pose
0°19/20   95%19/20   95%
90°7/20   35%20/20   100%
135°5/20   25%20/20   100%
180°12/20   60%19/20   95%
Why post-processing cannot save you

At 135° of roll the pose estimator returns nothing on three quarters of frames. There are no landmarks to normalise, smooth, re-project or reason about. Any architecture that assumes it will receive noisy-but-present landmarks is solving the wrong problem.

Two distinct failures hide in one sentence

Failure A — the detector's prior. Pose models are trained on photographs of people who are standing, sitting or lying on a planet. The network encodes “heads are near the top” as a prior. Rotate the person and the prior fights the evidence.

Failure B — the reference frame. Even with perfect landmarks, “hand 1.2 m above the floor” is meaningless when there is no floor. The only stable reference in the module is the equipment itself.

03

What we already solved, and its exact ceiling

Both failures have shipped fixes. One of them only covers half the rotation space, and that half is the part nobody has tested.

Failure A is answered by pipeline/upright_pose.py. Rather than asking the pose model to cope with an unusual orientation, we remove the orientation. The payload rack's own roll is read from the HSV detector's minimum-area rectangle, the entire frame is rotated until the rack is level, MediaPipe runs on the straightened image, and the returned landmarks are rotated back into original image coordinates.

Failure B is answered by pipeline/rack_frame.py. Landmarks are re-expressed in the rack's reference frame, so the system reasons about hand-to-container and body-to-rack instead of head-to-sky and feet-to-floor. That is requirement 2, satisfied.

The ceiling, stated plainly

Our 2D solution is orientation-agnostic with respect to roll. It is not orientation-agnostic with respect to pitch and yaw. And because every rendered take varies roll only, we currently have no evidence either way about how the system behaves on the half of the rotation space it does not handle.

This is precisely the gap 3D HMR is supposed to fill, and it is why the plan in section 07 starts by generating the missing data rather than by installing a model.

04

Rotation representation decides this before the model does

An astronaut occupies the entire rotation space. Two of the three common ways to write down a rotation break somewhere inside that space.

A network that outputs or consumes body orientation has to encode a rotation as numbers. That encoding is not a formatting detail. Get it wrong and the network has to learn across a discontinuity that does not exist in the physical world.

Euler angles: two separate defects

Three sequential rotations about three axes, for example R = Rz(γ) · Ry(β) · Rx(α). Compact and readable, and it fails twice over.

A crew member drifting in a module passes through these configurations routinely. On Earth you can often arrange your axes so the singularity sits somewhere nobody goes. In microgravity there is no such somewhere.

Quaternions: no lock, but an ambiguous target

Four numbers on the unit sphere. Gimbal lock disappears. A different problem replaces it: q and −q represent the same physical rotation. Every orientation has two equally valid encodings that look nothing alike numerically.

q = ( 0.71, 0.00, 0.71, 0.00 ) ← same rotation −q = (−0.71, −0.00, −0.71, −0.00 ) ← same rotation A mean-squared-error loss sees these as maximally different. Two training frames one millisecond apart can land on opposite signs, and the network is punished for being right.

You can patch this by canonicalising the sign, but the patch has its own discontinuity at the boundary where the canonical choice flips.

The 6D continuous representation: what modern pose networks use

Take the 3×3 rotation matrix and keep only its first two columns. Six numbers, no constraints, no ambiguity. The third column is redundant because it is fully determined by the first two — it is their cross product.

Decision

Every rotation that enters or leaves a network in this project is carried as 6D continuous. Euler angles are permitted only in human-facing output — a HUD readout or a log line — where a person reads the number and no gradient flows through it.

05

The architecture

How a raw frame becomes a rack-relative 3D body, and what the classifier actually receives.

HMR does not measure depth. A single camera physically cannot. What it does is fit a parametric human body model — SMPL, roughly ten shape numbers and twenty-four joint rotations — to the image, and report the body whose projection best explains those pixels. The output is a plausible 3D body, not a measured one.

The composition that satisfies the requirement

// HMR gives the body's orientation in camera coordinates R_body = rotation of the crew member, from the SMPL fit // the rack is fixed and the camera is fixed, so this is calibration R_rack = rotation of the payload rack, measured once at install // compose to get orientation with respect to the equipment R_rel = R_rackᵀ · R_body // carry it into the network as six continuous numbers rot6d = first two columns of R_rel

That is the literal content of “track the astronaut's body relative to the payload rack, not the floor.” Today the network has to infer orientation implicitly from landmark positions. Here it is handed over explicitly, in a form with no discontinuities.

Objects are still 2D, and the rack solves it anyway

An honest limitation: HMR reconstructs the body. The red and yellow boxes come from HSV in two dimensions, so “is the hand in front of or behind the box” is not directly answerable. But the question that actually determines the protocol step is different, and it is answerable.

What the classifier receives

Do not reshape HMR output into MediaPipe's 132-slot format. That is what the current adapter does, and it discards the single most valuable output — global orientation — while leaving nine of thirty-three slots empty. Build a vector designed around what HMR actually produces.

72 24 SMPL joints × 3, root-relative metres — body configuration HMR
6 Body rotation relative to the rack, 6D continuous — the requirement, as a number HMR × calib
6 Angular velocity of that rotation, finite difference across the window derived
6 Both hands in rack coordinates, including signed distance to the rack plane HMR × twin
9 Three boxes × (centroid x, centroid y, visible) — absence encoded after normalisation HSV
2 Image-space distance from each projected hand to the nearest visible box HMR × HSV
101 Per frame, stacked into a 30-frame window for the sequence model —
The trap this design exists to avoid

The current adapter emits the same 132 numbers as MediaPipe, which means flipping HMR_BACKEND would feed root-relative metres into slots a trained model learned as image coordinates. Nothing would throw. Accuracy would simply fall, and the cause would look like anything except a configuration flag.

A distinct feature layout with a distinct dimension makes that mistake impossible to make silently: the model refuses to load against the wrong feature source.

06

What we build

Concrete files. Two exist and change; the rest are new.

pipeline/rotation.py new

The rotation vocabulary, in one place: matrix to 6D, 6D to matrix via Gram-Schmidt, rack-relative composition, and the Euler conversion used only for human-readable output. Everything else imports from here so no second convention can appear.

pipeline/rack_calibration.py new

Measure and store the rack's pose in camera coordinates once, at install. Loads a JSON calibration; refuses to run with a stale one when the camera has moved. In training this comes free from the digital twin, which knows exactly where the rack is.

pipeline/hmr_backend.py modify

Stop impersonating MediaPipe. Return the SMPL joints, the global rotation and the weak-perspective camera as a structured result instead of flattening into 132 slots. Keep the existing graceful fallback when no HMR package is installed.

pipeline/features.py modify

Already planned as the single feature builder shared by training and inference. Gains a second layout: the 101-dim HMR vector alongside the existing 143-dim 2D vector, with the active layout recorded in the checkpoint so a mismatch fails loudly at load.

blender/render_dataset.py modify

Add pitch and yaw takes. Yaw is nearly free — facing_deg is already plumbed through build_scene.py and simply never set. Pitch needs the astronaut builder's hardcoded zero X rotation opened up.

tests/test_rotation.py new

Round-trip property tests: matrix → 6D → matrix recovers the original to numerical tolerance, across the full rotation space including the attitudes where Euler and quaternions misbehave. Cheap to write, and it is the foundation everything else stands on.

07

The plan, with gates

Each phase has to earn the next one. The first gate costs under an hour and can cancel the remaining four phases.

HMR is expensive: a heavy dependency, a model on a 6 GB card, latency in the live loop, and a retraining cycle. It should be adopted because a measurement demanded it, not because a document suggested it.

PHASE 0

Generate the missing half of the rotation space

Add pitch and yaw variation to the render plan. Yaw is one line — facing_deg is plumbed end to end and never set. Pitch needs a scene-API change where the X rotation is hardcoded to zero. Tag them so they can be held out separately.

human ~4 h · CC ~30 min · no additional GPU hours

PHASE 1

Measure whether the 2D stack actually breaks

Run the existing upright_pose and rack_frame path over the new pitch and yaw takes. Report detection rate and held-out step accuracy, exactly as the roll table in section 02.

human ~2 h · CC ~20 min

GATE. If 2D holds up at pitch and yaw, HMR is optional polish. Ship the measured 2D solution, document the negative result, and stop here. If 2D degrades, HMR has a measured job and the remaining phases are justified work.
PHASE 2

Backend and contract

Install an HMR package, prove it runs offline on the RTX 3050, then verify the SMPL joint mapping empirically rather than by reading the table — this mapping has been wrong once already. Pin the coordinate and visibility conventions in writing.

human ~1 day · CC ~2 h · agents: hmr-env, hmr-contract

RISK. SMPL models human body shape. The crew wears a pressure suit with roughly 7 cm of padding, which already distorts MediaPipe by 101 px. An EVA suit is out of distribution for models trained on ordinary photographs. Measure this before trusting anything downstream.
PHASE 3

Rotation module, calibration, and the 101-dim vector

Build rotation.py with its round-trip tests first, then the rack calibration, then the feature layout. Add an HMR pose source to the sequence builder so training and serving see the same features. Retrain.

human ~2 days · CC ~1.5 h · agent: hmr-training

PHASE 4

The comparison that decides it

HMR against 2D on identical splits, identical windows, including the pitch and yaw takes. Latency and VRAM measured alongside, because a model that wins on accuracy and loses the real-time budget has not won.

human ~1 day · CC ~1 h · agents: hmr-eval, hmr-perf

GATE. HMR ships only if it beats 2D on the held-out number and fits the latency budget. A negative result here is a finding worth publishing, not a failure worth hiding.
PHASE 5

Integration behind a flag

Wire it in with 2D as the permanent fallback. A mid-session HMR failure degrades to 2D visibly and without a crash; a checkpoint trained on one feature source refuses to load against the other; the dashboard states which backend is live.

human ~1 day · CC ~45 min · agent: hmr-integration

08

What we would say about this

The defensible position, whichever way the gates fall.

The problem statement predicts that ground-referenced pose models fail in microgravity. We tested the prediction rather than accepting it: MediaPipe alone detects a crew member rolled 135° in five frames out of twenty. We solved it by canonicalising the image using the payload rack's own roll before pose estimation, taking detection to twenty out of twenty, and by expressing every landmark in the rack's reference frame rather than the world's.

3D HMR extends that to out-of-plane attitude, where image rotation cannot help, and it carries body orientation relative to the rack as an explicit continuous input rather than something the network must infer. It is implemented behind a flag with the joint mapping verified, and it ships if and only if it beats the 2D path on the same held-out takes within the edge latency budget.

Why this is the stronger answer

An unmeasured HMR integration demonstrates that you read the problem statement. A measured comparison, with a stated rotation representation and a gate that can reject your own work, demonstrates that you understood it.

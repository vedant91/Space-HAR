01

The gap we found, and the three bets we made

Before writing a line of the pipeline, we pulled every public repository against this problem statement — 22 of them — and read the architecture each one shipped.

Every one of the 22 shares the same shape: a webcam pointed at someone sitting upright at a desk, YOLO or HSV colour blobs for the objects, MediaPipe for hands, a hand-written rule layer, and a dashboard. None of them:

  • tests a rotated or inverted subject — every demo video is someone standing normally in a normal room
  • trains or evaluates on more than one camera angle
  • runs a real 3D human-mesh-recovery model — several mention it in a slide, none ship it
  • reports a held-out accuracy number computed by video/take rather than by shuffled frame (a split that silently leaks information between train and test)

A person in microgravity doesn't stay upright. That's not an edge case for this problem statement — it's the actual problem.

BET 01

A digital twin, not a webcam

Blender renders the astronaut, the rack, and the props at any roll / pitch / yaw / lighting combination we choose — orientations no phone camera in a dorm room can produce, with exact 3D ground truth for free.

BET 02

Train on some angles, test on others

Every evaluation number on this page comes from takes held out by whole recording, never by frame — so a model can't memorise a background pixel and call it generalisation.

BET 03

Ship real HMR, not a slide about it

A trained 3D body-pose network runs live in the pipeline and measurably beats the pretrained model it replaced, on our own honest test.

02

End-to-end architecture

One fixed camera in, five outputs out. Everything in between is on this page.

Same shape whether HMR is confident or not — the fusion gate is what makes the 3D branch an addition, never a single point of failure. Lose HMR entirely and the state machine still runs on boxes and 2D hands.

How HMR actually gets a 3D body out of one 2D camera

Human mesh recovery is the one component every competitor mentions and none ship. Here's the pipeline that made it real, and the honest numbers once it was measured rather than assumed.

The honest before/after — pretrained ROMP vs. our own trained model

Hypothesis confirmed, partially. The zero-tracked takes weren't a hard information limit — they were a training-data gap. We rendered 10 more takes at 40°/50°/65°/75°/85° roll (both directions) and retrained: 60° roll went from completely untracked (0.00) to 0.77 — the single largest jump on this page — and −90° moved from 0.00 to 0.22. Filling in the angles the model had never seen made them learnable. But exactly 90° roll stayed stuck (0.07, and 0.02 under dim light) even with 75° and 85° now in the training set either side of it — MediaPipe's own 2D tracking is still reliable there (0.90–1.00 hit-rate), so this isn't a framing bug either. At precisely edge-on to a single fixed camera, one whole side of the body is almost entirely self-occluded — that looks like a genuine information limit of a single camera at that exact angle, not something more training data alone fixes. A second camera angle, or accepting the 2D fallback for that narrow band, are the remaining options.

05

Training pipeline

Everything a model sees live, it also saw in training — same code, same feature builder, enforced by a loaded-model contract check that refuses to serve a mismatched pair.

01 · sources

Blender digital twin

33 takes, every orientation, lighting and clutter condition, exact 3D ground truth for free

02 · labels

Auto-exported YOLO boxes

Every visible box in every frame, straight from the render metadata — zero manual annotation

03 · sequence build

Run the trained detector

Features are built from the detector's real, imperfect output — never the ground truth boxes

04 · ship

Contract-checked promotion

promote_model.py refuses to deploy a checkpoint and ONNX pair whose feature shapes disagree

Held out by take, never by frame, throughout: a model can't pass by memorising a pixel of background that happens to repeat between two frames of the same clip. This one discipline is what makes every number on this page mean what it says.

08

Why this wins the problem statement, not just a demo

What we found in 22 public repos

  • Trained and tested on an upright, seated subject in a normal room
  • Object detection via colour blobs or an off-the-shelf detector, never retrained on the actual props
  • HMR mentioned as future work, never shipped
  • Accuracy reported from a frame-level split, or not reported at all
  • One fixed reference frame: the camera itself

What we shipped

  • Trained and evaluated across roll/pitch/yaw from 0° to full inversion
  • A detector trained from scratch on the exact rack and boxes, mAP@50 0.934
  • A working 3D body model, trained by us, running live, honestly scored
  • Every accuracy number held out by take, reproducible from disk
  • A rack-anchored reference frame — "in the target zone" means something a body-only frame can't express
09

Key engineering decisions

The parts a demo video won't show, but a judge's question will find.

01

Entropy-gated step confirmation

A confidence threshold alone can't tell a decisive call from a coin flip that happened to clear the bar. We gate on the full softmax's Shannon entropy alongside confidence, re-derived for our own 9-class problem rather than reused from a competitor's 8-class number.

02

Model-contract guard

The live pipeline reads the feature layout, sample rate, and rack-normalisation flag a checkpoint was actually trained with, and refuses to serve it against a mismatched feature builder — the exact failure mode that once shipped a model that scored at chance.

03

Rack-relative reference frame

Every position is expressed relative to the rack, not the camera or the astronaut's own body — so "box placed in the target zone" and "crew attitude relative to the rack" are both expressible, which a body-centred frame cannot do.

04

Adaptive HMR scheduling

The 3D model only re-runs when the tracked torso angle has drifted past 15° or a second has passed — holding still to examine a box costs nothing, a fast tumble gets checked often.

05

Graceful 2D fallback, always on

The fusion gate falls back to MediaPipe's 2D pose the instant HMR is unsure, so step recognition keeps working even in the orientations no vision model handles perfectly yet.

06

Honest evaluation discipline, enforced by test

Held-out-by-take splitting isn't a one-off — it's checked by unit tests that assert no take ID appears in both a training and validation split, on every model this project trains.
